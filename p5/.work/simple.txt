Jigsaw puzzles began in the 1760s when a London mapmaker glued maps on a wooden board and cut around the borders of each country with a small saw. Teachers used these puzzles to help children learn the names and shapes of the lands of the world. For many years the puzzles were made by hand, one at a time, so only rich families could buy them. When machines learned to press pictures onto thick card and stamp out hundreds of pieces at once, the price fell and the puzzles spread to every town. During hard times in the 1930s people rented puzzles for a few cents a night because they were a cheap way to pass a long evening. Some makers cut their pieces along the lines of colour so that there were no easy clues, and a few even left out the picture on the box. Today the largest puzzles have more than fifty thousand pieces and can take a patient family many months to finish, yet the idea is still the same as it was in that small shop long ago.

Kites were first flown in China more than two thousand years ago. The early kites were made of silk and bamboo, which were light and strong, and they were often shaped like birds or dragons. Soldiers used them to send signals over long distances and to measure how far away an enemy camp stood. One story tells of a general who flew a kite over the walls of a city to work out how long a tunnel his men would need to dig. Kites later travelled along the trade roads to Korea, Japan and India, and each land gave them new shapes and new games. In some places people glued tiny pieces of glass to the string and tried to cut the lines of other kites in the sky. Much later, men of science used kites to study the weather, to lift cameras and to learn how wings behave in the wind. The brothers who built the first working aeroplane spent years flying large kites on a windy beach before they dared to add an engine. Children still run across fields with kites today, and the simple toy has hardly changed at all.

The first bicycles had no pedals. A rider sat on a wooden frame between two wheels and pushed along the ground with both feet, a little like running while sitting down. These early machines were heavy and had no brakes, so going down a hill was a real adventure. About fifty years later someone fixed pedals to the front wheel, and to go faster the makers kept making that wheel bigger and bigger. The result was the famous high wheeler, on which the rider sat far above the road and could be thrown over the handlebars by a single stone. The safety bicycle, with two wheels of the same size and a chain to drive the back one, arrived in the 1880s and looked very much like the bicycles we ride now. Air filled tyres soon followed and made the ride smooth at last. For the first time ordinary people could travel many miles in a day without a horse, and the bicycle changed where they could work, shop and visit. Many of the first car makers began their working lives in small bicycle shops.

Honey bees live together in a large family called a colony. In the middle of summer a healthy colony may hold fifty thousand bees, and almost all of them are workers. The workers are females that do not lay eggs. Young workers stay inside the hive, where they clean the cells, feed the young and build new comb from wax that they make in their own bodies. Older workers guard the door and then fly out to collect sweet nectar and yellow pollen from flowers. When a bee finds a good field of flowers she returns to the hive and performs a little dance on the comb. The direction and the length of her dance tell the other bees where to fly and how far the food is. There is only one queen in each colony, and her main task is to lay eggs, sometimes more than a thousand in a single day. The male bees are called drones, and they do no work in the hive at all. In autumn, when food is hard to find, the workers push the drones out into the cold.

Tea is made from the leaves of a small evergreen bush that grows best on warm, wet hillsides. According to an old legend, a Chinese emperor discovered the drink almost five thousand years ago when a few leaves blew into a pot of water that his servant was boiling. For a long time tea was taken as a medicine, and only later did people begin to drink it for pleasure. Monks carried the plant to Japan, where the making and serving of tea grew into a quiet and careful ceremony. Dutch and English ships brought the first chests of tea to Europe in the 1600s, and at first it was so costly that families kept it in locked boxes. As more ships sailed, the price came down, and tea with milk and sugar became the daily drink of working people in Britain. Green tea and black tea come from the same plant; the difference lies in how long the leaves are left to dry and darken before they are heated. Today tea is the most popular drink in the world after plain water.

Lighthouses have guided sailors for more than two thousand years. The most famous of the ancient ones stood on a small island near the city of Alexandria in Egypt, and a fire burned at its top every night. For centuries the light of a lighthouse came from burning wood or coal, and later from oil lamps with many wicks. The great change came in the 1820s, when a French engineer designed a lens made of rings of glass that gathered the light into a single strong beam. With this lens a lamp could be seen from more than twenty miles away. Each lighthouse was given its own pattern of flashes so that a captain could tell which part of the coast he was passing. The keepers who looked after the lamps often lived with their families on lonely rocks, and in bad weather they might see no visitors for weeks. Almost all lighthouses now work by themselves, with electric lamps that switch on at dusk, but ships still watch for them even in the age of satellites.

Chocolate comes from the seeds of the cacao tree, which grows only in hot and rainy lands near the equator. The people of ancient Mexico crushed the seeds, mixed them with water, pepper and spices, and drank the bitter liquid cold. The seeds were so valuable that they were also used as money, and a turkey could be bought for a hundred of them. Spanish sailors carried cacao home in the 1500s, where cooks added sugar and served the drink hot to kings and queens. For almost three hundred years chocolate remained something to drink. Then, in the 1800s, a Dutch chemist found a way to press the fat out of the seeds, and an English company learned to mix the fat back in to make a solid bar that could be eaten. A Swiss maker later added dried milk and produced the first milk chocolate. Today most cacao is grown on small family farms in West Africa, and it takes the seeds of about four hundred pods to make a single pound of chocolate.

The pencil is one of the simplest writing tools ever made, yet it has a curious history. In the 1500s a storm in the north of England pulled up a large tree, and shepherds found a strange black rock among its roots. They used it to mark their sheep. The rock was graphite, although people of the time thought it was a kind of lead, and the name has stuck ever since. Sticks of graphite were soon wrapped in string or pressed between two strips of wood so that writers could keep their fingers clean. When war cut off the supply of English graphite, a French officer discovered that powdered graphite could be mixed with clay and baked in an oven. More clay gave a hard, pale line and less clay gave a soft, dark one, which is why pencils today are sold in many grades. The small rubber at the end was added in America in 1858. A single ordinary pencil can draw a line more than thirty miles long, and it will even write upside down or under water.

Rivers begin as small streams high in the hills, where rain and melting snow run down the slopes. As the streams join together the river grows wider and stronger, and it cuts a valley through the land over thousands of years. Fast water in the mountains can roll large stones along the bed, and the stones grind each other into pebbles and then into sand. Lower down, where the ground is flat, the river slows and swings from side to side in great bends. In times of flood it spreads fine mud across the fields, and this mud makes some of the richest farm land on earth. That is why so many of the first cities were built on the banks of rivers such as the Nile and the Indus. At the end of its journey the river meets the sea and drops the last of its sand, which may build up into a wide fan of new land called a delta. People have always used rivers for drinking water, for travel and for turning the wheels of mills.

Paper was invented in China about two thousand years ago by an official of the royal court. Before that time people wrote on strips of bamboo, which were heavy, or on silk, which cost a great deal. The new material was made by soaking old rags, tree bark and fishing nets in water, beating them into a soft pulp and spreading the pulp thinly on a screen to dry in the sun. The secret stayed in China for several hundred years. It reached the Arab world after a battle in which some paper makers were taken prisoner, and from there it slowly passed into Spain and Italy. Paper was far cheaper than the animal skins that had been used for books in Europe, and when the printing press arrived the two inventions together made books common for the first time. Until the 1800s nearly all paper was made from rags, and there were never enough of them. Then makers learned to use wood, and huge mills began to turn whole forests into newspapers.

Clocks did not always have hands. The oldest way to tell the time was to watch the shadow of a stick move across the ground as the sun crossed the sky. This worked well on bright days but was useless at night, so people also measured time with water that dripped slowly from one bowl into another, or with candles marked in rings. The first mechanical clocks appeared in the towers of European churches about seven hundred years ago. They were driven by falling weights and had no faces at all; they simply rang a bell every hour to call the monks to prayer. Springs made it possible to build clocks small enough to stand on a table and, later, to carry in a pocket. A Dutch scientist added the swinging pendulum in 1656, and clocks suddenly became accurate to within a few seconds a day. Sailors needed even better clocks to find their position at sea, and an English carpenter spent most of his life building one that could keep time on a rolling ship.

The potato was first grown in the high mountains of South America, where farmers have raised it for at least seven thousand years. They learned to freeze the potatoes in the cold night air and dry them in the sun, which made a light food that could be stored for years. Spanish ships carried the plant to Europe in the 1500s, but for a long time people there refused to eat it. Some thought it caused illness because it belonged to the same family as several poisonous plants. Slowly farmers discovered that a small field of potatoes could feed a family far better than the same field of wheat. In Ireland the poor came to depend on it almost completely, and when a disease destroyed the crop in the 1840s about a million people died of hunger and a million more left the country. Today the potato is grown in more than a hundred countries, and only rice, wheat and maize feed more people. There are thousands of kinds, with skins that may be brown, red, yellow or purple.

Glass is made by melting sand with ash and lime in a very hot furnace. Nobody knows who first discovered it, but beads of coloured glass were being made in Egypt more than four thousand years ago. For a long time glass was as precious as jewels. Then workers on the coast of Syria found that a lump of hot glass on the end of a hollow iron pipe could be blown up like a bubble and shaped into cups, bottles and bowls. This quick method made glass cheap enough for ordinary homes across the Roman world. Flat glass for windows was much harder to make. For centuries it was spun into round discs and cut into small panes, which is why old windows are made of so many little pieces held together with strips of lead. In the 1950s an English company learned to float melted glass on a bath of liquid tin, which gave large sheets that were perfectly flat on both sides. Nearly every window in the world is now made in this way.

Salt was once so important that roads were built to carry it and wars were fought to control it. Before there were tins or freezers, salt was the best way to keep meat and fish from going bad, so a town without salt could not store food for the winter. Roman soldiers were sometimes paid part of their wages in salt, and the word salary comes from this custom. In many countries the ruler held the only right to sell salt and placed a heavy tax on it. Most salt was won from the sea by letting water dry in shallow ponds under the sun, but some was dug from deep mines where ancient seas had dried up long ago. One famous mine in Poland was worked for seven hundred years, and the miners carved whole rooms and statues out of the grey rock salt. Today salt is cheap and plentiful, and far more of it is spread on icy roads and used in factories than is ever sprinkled on food.
